Tariffs Are Back — But Nothing Is Law Yet

The US has proposed a new round of tariffs on 14 economies, including Singapore. Marcus and Maya explain why nothing is law yet, how the Section 301 process works, and why Wednesday's sharp market sell-off almost disappeared by the close.

Morning news•Two hosts•Approximately 10 minutes
Read the full episode transcript

On 25 November 2026 the US proposed new tariffs on 14 economies — China up to 20%, Singapore up to 20% or 10% under an agreement — rebuilt under Section 301 after the Supreme Court struck down the emergency tariffs. Nothing takes effect yet, and Wednesday's market panic mostly melted away by the close.

Marcus: Welcome to the show. Today we are talking about tariffs, because on Wednesday, November twenty fifth, twenty twenty six, the United States proposed a whole new round of them, and the market reaction was wild.

Maya: And this is not a small story. It is about fourteen economies, a court defeat from earlier this year, and a market that sold off hard in the morning and then almost completely recovered by the close. So let us start with what actually happened.

Marcus: Here are the facts first. On November twenty fifth, the Office of the United States Trade Representative, known as the U S T R, said it had found that fourteen economies had manufacturing policies that were unreasonable and burdened United States commerce. It proposed additional tariffs on goods from each of them.

Maya: Who is on the list?

Marcus: China, Bangladesh, Cambodia, the European Union, India, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Taiwan, Thailand, and Vietnam. Mexico and Norway were investigated too, but no tariffs were proposed for them at this time.

Maya: And the rates are not all the same, right?

Marcus: No, they vary a lot. China would face up to twenty percent. Vietnam would face up to twenty percent with a trade deal, and up to thirty five percent without one. India would face up to twenty five percent, or seventeen percent if a trade deal is finalised. Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia, and Thailand would face up to eighteen percent. The European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and Switzerland would face fifteen percent. Singapore would face up to twenty percent, or ten percent under a qualifying agreement.

Maya: That Singapore detail matters for a lot of listeners here, because Singapore is on the list. But I want to be clear on one thing. None of this is law yet, is it?

Marcus: That is right, and it is the most important fact in this episode. Nothing takes effect yet. Written comments are due on January fifth, twenty twenty seven. A hearing is scheduled for January twenty fifth, twenty twenty seven, at ten o clock in the morning. Implementation could come in January at the earliest, and only after that process.

Maya: So why is the administration doing this now, and why under this particular law?

Marcus: To answer that, we have to go back to February. On February twentieth, twenty twenty six, the United States Supreme Court struck down the sweeping global tariffs that had been imposed under an emergency powers law. The vote was six to three. The court said the president had exceeded his authority, because the Constitution gives Congress the power to impose taxes, and tariffs are taxes.

Maya: So the old route was closed, and the administration needed a new legal route.

Marcus: Exactly. The new route is Section three hundred one of the Trade Act of nineteen seventy four. That law lets the U S T R investigate foreign practices it judges to be unreasonable and a burden on United States commerce, and then propose retaliatory tariffs. It is a tool presidents from both parties have used before, including in the first Trump administration, and those earlier tariffs survived court challenges.

Maya: And this investigation was not rushed.

Marcus: No. The U S T R opened it on March eleventh, twenty twenty six, against sixteen economies. That is about eight months of investigation before the determinations on November twenty fifth. The official reason given is what Washington calls structural excess capacity, basically the claim that these economies produce too much, flood world markets with cheap exports, and hurt United States manufacturers.

Maya: Okay, so that is the legal story. Now the market story, because Wednesday was a strange day.

Marcus: It was. The tariff news landed on the same morning as two other big releases. October core P C E inflation, which is the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation measure, rose zero point three percent in the month, and three point three percent over the year, slightly above the three point two percent that was expected. Headline P C E was three point eight percent over the year, in line with expectations.

Maya: And growth was strong too.

Marcus: Very strong. Third quarter G D P was revised up to four point three percent, from three point four percent, and above the three point five percent that economists expected. So you had hot inflation and hot growth on the same morning, which made a December rate hike look even more likely.

Maya: And then the market fell.

Marcus: In the morning, it fell hard. The S and P five hundred was down as much as three point two percent intraday. The Nasdaq Composite was down almost four percent by midday, and nearly four point six percent at its low. The Dow was down more than one thousand points at one stage. Gold fell too, by about three percent to around three thousand four hundred and fifty five dollars, instead of rising as a safe haven.

Maya: But by the close, the picture looked very different.

Marcus: It did. Buyers stepped in during the afternoon. By the close, the S and P five hundred was down only zero point two five percent, at seven thousand one hundred and eighty three. The Nasdaq was down zero point six two percent. The Dow actually closed up zero point three three percent, above fifty thousand. The Russell two thousand was up zero point seven one percent.

Maya: That is a huge intraday swing. What do you make of it?

Marcus: Let us separate fact from opinion here, because that matters. The facts are the proposal, the rates, the dates, and the market moves we just gave you. What follows is our opinion, not a forecast, and not financial advice.

Maya: Good, let us do that. What is your opinion?

Marcus: My opinion is that the market first reacted to the headline, and then remembered the timeline. A proposal is not a tariff. There are weeks of comments, lobbying, negotiations, and possible carve outs before anything can take effect. Companies and countries will push back. Rates may change. Some products will be exempt. That is why I think the morning sell off was more about fear and thin trading before Thanksgiving than about a final cost that businesses can already calculate.

Maya: I agree with that, and I would add a second opinion. The legal change matters more than the headline rate. The February court ruling forced the administration to build a case economy by economy, under a law that has survived before. That does not make these tariffs certain, and they will almost certainly be challenged in court, but it does make them harder to dismiss as a one day headline. In my view, that is why traders should watch the process, not just the percentages.

Marcus: And there is a third point I think is underappreciated. This landed on a day when inflation was already hot and growth was already strong. If tariffs eventually raise import costs, that could keep inflation higher for longer, while also hurting growth. That is a difficult mix for the Federal Reserve, because higher inflation points to tighter policy, while weaker growth points to looser policy. I am not saying that will happen. I am saying that tension is why the market was so nervous.

Maya: Let us bring it back to Singapore for a moment, because that is where many listeners are.

Marcus: Singapore is proposed at up to twenty percent, or ten percent under a qualifying agreement. We do not yet know which products would be covered, or which would be exempt, because each economy has its own notice with its own product lists. So anyone trying to work out the exact impact on a Singapore exporter right now is guessing. The honest position is to wait for the product lists and the comment process.

Maya: What dates should listeners actually put in their diary?

Marcus: Three dates. January fifth, twenty twenty seven, for written comments. January twenty fifth, twenty twenty seven, for the hearing. And January tenth, when the current United States China trade truce is due to expire. Those are the next real decision points, not this week.

Maya: So what is the takeaway?

Marcus: The takeaway is this. On November twenty fifth, the United States proposed new tariffs on fourteen economies, with China at up to twenty percent, the European Union, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and Switzerland at fifteen percent, and Singapore at up to twenty percent or ten percent under an agreement. The proposal follows an eight month investigation under Section three hundred one, after the Supreme Court struck down the earlier emergency tariffs in February. Nothing is in force yet, and the comment period and hearing run into January.

Maya: And our opinion is that the morning panic told you more about timing and thin holiday trading than about the final tariffs, but the legal rebuild tells you this story is not going away quickly. Watch the process, watch the product lists, and watch January.

Marcus: That is it for today. If you found this useful, the next episode to watch for is what actually gets filed in those January comments, because that is when we will learn which industries are fighting hardest, and where the rates might move. Thanks for listening.

Key takeaways

  • Nothing is law yet: on 25 Nov 2026 the USTR proposed tariffs on 14 economies — written comments are due 5 Jan 2027, the hearing is 25 Jan 2027, and implementation could come in January at the earliest.
  • Rates vary a lot by economy: China up to 20%; Vietnam up to 20% with a trade deal, 35% without; India up to 25%, or 17% with a deal; Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand up to 18%; the EU, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Switzerland 15%; Singapore up to 20%, or 10% under a qualifying agreement.
  • The legal route changed: after the Supreme Court struck down the emergency-powers tariffs 6–3 on 20 Feb 2026, the administration rebuilt its case under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, following an investigation opened 11 Mar 2026 into 16 economies (Mexico and Norway got no proposed tariffs).
  • Wednesday's whipsaw: the S&P 500 fell as much as 3.2% intraday, the Nasdaq nearly 4.6% at its low and the Dow over 1,000 points, yet the S&P closed down just 0.25% and the Dow up 0.33% — on a morning that also brought hot core PCE (3.3% year-on-year) and Q3 GDP revised up to 4.3%.
  • Opinion, clearly labelled as such: the morning panic said more about headline fear and thin pre-Thanksgiving trading than a final tariff cost — but the Section 301 rebuild makes this harder to dismiss, so watch the process and the product lists, not just the percentages.

Questions to consider

  • Which industries will fight hardest in the January comments — and where might the proposed rates actually move?
  • If tariffs eventually raise import costs while growth stays strong, does the Fed end up facing higher inflation and weaker growth at once — and which way does it lean?
  • For Singapore, which products end up covered or exempt once the product lists are out — and does Singapore land at 20% or the 10% agreement rate?

Jensen Huang Is Back at NVIDIA — What the Board Said, and What It Didn't Settle

NVIDIA's board has reinstated Jensen Huang, but the investigation behind his return remains open. We unpack what the board confirmed, the government input it cited, and the oversight changes that leave important questions still to be answered.

Uploaded: 25 November 2026•Morning news•Solo•Approximately 1 minute
Read the full episode transcript

Jensen Huang is back in charge at NVIDIA effective immediately, but the investigation behind his return is still open, and here's what the board actually said.

NVIDIA's Board has voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang as President and CEO, effective 23 November. The release describes government input on national security as part of the decision. But it also leaves an investigation open — and that gap between what was decided and what is still unresolved is the real story in this episode.

Alongside the reinstatement, NVIDIA is setting up a new National Security and Export Control Committee, and has begun a search for a Chief Compliance Officer. So the headline is a return; the detail is a company adding oversight at the very top while questions remain unanswered.

Key takeaways

  • Jensen Huang was reinstated effective November 23, 2026, as President, CEO and Board director after a unanimous Board vote.
  • The Board said it was satisfied with Huang's candor and ability to lead, and that, based on input from senior levels of the U.S. government, it is confident his reinstatement poses no national security risk.
  • Colette Kress was thanked for her service as interim CEO and continues as Chief Financial Officer, while Harvey C. Jones remains Board chair.
  • The independent directors' investigation prompted by reporting that Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort will proceed to conclusion, with Huang supporting it and committed to cooperating.
  • NVIDIA has formed a National Security and Export Control Committee of independent directors and begun a search for a new Chief Compliance Officer, and says it will continue cooperating with ongoing government investigations and inquiries.

Questions to consider

  • What investigation is still open, and what does the release say — and not say — about the reported foreign intelligence effort?
  • What new oversight has NVIDIA created, including the National Security and Export Control Committee and the new Chief Compliance Officer role?
