Tariffs Are Back — But Nothing Is Law Yet
Morning news · Two hosts · ~10 min

On 25 November 2026 the US proposed new tariffs on 14 economies — China up to 20%, Singapore up to 20% or 10% under an agreement — rebuilt under Section 301 after the Supreme Court struck down the emergency tariffs. Nothing takes effect yet, and Wednesday's market panic mostly melted away by the close.

Maya: So why is the administration doing this now, and why under this particular law?

On February twentieth, twenty twenty six, the United States Supreme Court struck down the sweeping global tariffs that had been imposed under an emergency powers law. The vote was six to three. The court said the president had exceeded his authority, because the Constitution gives Congress the power to impose taxes, and tariffs are taxes.
Maya: So the old route was closed, and the administration needed a new legal route.
Marcus: Exactly. The new route is Section three hundred one of the Trade Act of nineteen seventy four. That law lets the U S T R investigate foreign practices it judges to be unreasonable and a burden on United States commerce, and then propose retaliatory tariffs. It is a tool presidents from both parties have used before, including in the first Trump administration, and those earlier tariffs survived court challenges.
Maya: And this investigation was not rushed.
Marcus: No. The U S T R opened it on March eleventh, twenty twenty six, against sixteen economies. That is about eight months of investigation before the determinations on November twenty fifth. The official reason given is what Washington calls structural excess capacity, basically the claim that these economies produce too much, flood world markets with cheap exports, and hurt United States manufacturers.

Takeaways
- Nothing is law yet: on 25 Nov 2026 the USTR proposed tariffs on 14 economies — written comments are due 5 Jan 2027, the hearing is 25 Jan 2027, and implementation could come in January at the earliest.
- Rates vary a lot by economy: China up to 20%; Vietnam up to 20% with a trade deal, 35% without; India up to 25%, or 17% with a deal; Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand up to 18%; the EU, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Switzerland 15%; Singapore up to 20%, or 10% under a qualifying agreement.
- The legal route changed: after the Supreme Court struck down the emergency-powers tariffs 6–3 on 20 Feb 2026, the administration rebuilt its case under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, following an investigation opened 11 Mar 2026 into 16 economies (Mexico and Norway got no proposed tariffs).
- Wednesday's whipsaw: the S&P 500 fell as much as 3.2% intraday, the Nasdaq nearly 4.6% at its low and the Dow over 1,000 points, yet the S&P closed down just 0.25% and the Dow up 0.33% — on a morning that also brought hot core PCE (3.3% year-on-year) and Q3 GDP revised up to 4.3%.
- Opinion, clearly labelled as such: the morning panic said more about headline fear and thin pre-Thanksgiving trading than a final tariff cost — but the Section 301 rebuild makes this harder to dismiss, so watch the process and the product lists, not just the percentages.
